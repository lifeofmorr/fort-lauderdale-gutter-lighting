Before anyone measures

Who are you, and who's doing the work?

  1. 1

    Are you licensed and insured — and can I see it?

    Licensing for gutter work varies by city and county in Florida, so the practical ask is this: show me the license or registration that covers my job, and a current certificate of insurance for the business. Both should appear without hesitation or a follow-up promise. You can verify insurance with the insurer named on the certificate — legitimate contractors expect that call.

  2. 2

    Who actually does the installation?

    The person quoting is rarely the person installing. Ask whether the crew is the company's own or subcontracted, who supervises, and who you call if something's off on install day. "You'll deal with me" should have a name and a phone number attached.

  3. 3

    Will you inspect the fascia and soffit, or just the gutters?

    A guard fitted over rotted fascia is money spent decorating a problem. The inspection should include the wood the gutters hang from — and the honesty to tell you if repair comes first. It's one of the main things my South Florida gutter guide exists to explain.

The product and the paper

What exactly am I buying, and what guarantees it?

  1. 4

    What is the system, specifically?

    Full name, material, and how it filters. "A premium guard" is not a specification. Stainless micro-mesh, perforated aluminum, and a foam insert are wildly different products at wildly different lifespans — my guard comparison guide walks through all of them without a sales agenda.

  2. 5

    How does it attach — and does anything touch my roof?

    Ask whether fasteners go into the fascia, the gutter, or through shingles and roof deck — and then check what your roof warranty says about it. A straight answer here is specific and calm. Vagueness here is information, too.

  3. 6

    What's warrantied — product, labor, clogs — and who honors it?

    Three separate things can be covered: the product itself, the labor, and performance (what happens if it clogs anyway). Ask what's covered in each category, for how long, in writing — and who stands behind it if the local operation changes hands. A national company behind the installer is a different answer from a truck and a cell phone.

  4. 7

    Is the price exact and in writing?

    Ranges are for phone calls. Before you sign, the number should be one number, with the full scope listed — footage, downspouts, repair work, cleanup — so there's nothing left to "discover" mid-job.

The job itself

How does the work actually go?

  1. 8

    How long, and how many visits?

    Most straightforward homes are a single-day conversation; larger or repair-heavy rooflines aren't. What you want is a realistic answer, not the fastest one you could be told.

  2. 9

    Who cleans up and hauls the old material?

    Old gutters, cut-offs, and the debris that comes out of your system should leave with the crew. Confirm it, and confirm it's in the written scope.

  3. 10

    What's the payment schedule?

    A deposit structure tied to progress, with the balance at completion, is the healthy pattern. Everything up front, before a ladder touches your house, is the single clearest red flag on this page.

  4. 11

    What maintenance will it still need — honestly?

    The correct answer for every system ever made is "some, occasionally." Anyone who answers "none, ever" is telling you what closes the sale. A ground-level check after storm season is the realistic standard for a quality system.

  5. 12

    If something's wrong in year two, who do I call?

    Not "the warranty department" — a who. Local presence plus a company that will still exist are the two halves of a warranty being worth the paper it's printed on.

Questions

Hiring, answered

Do gutter contractors in Florida need a license?

It varies — licensing requirements for gutter work differ by city and county in Florida, and there isn't a single statewide gutter license to ask for by number. What you can always ask for, and should: the business's license or registration for your locality, and a current certificate of insurance naming the business. A legitimate contractor produces both without friction, and you can verify coverage with the insurer.

Should a gutter quote be free?

Yes, and it should include an actual inspection. A real gutter quote requires measuring the roofline and checking the fascia and soffit — a price given over the phone, sight unseen, is a guess dressed as a quote. Expect the inspection and the written quote to cost you nothing and obligate you to nothing.

Is full payment up front a red flag?

Yes. Full payment before work begins removes every bit of your leverage and is not how established companies operate. A deposit structure tied to the work — with the balance due at completion — is the normal, healthy pattern. Get the schedule in the written quote, whatever company you choose.
