Homeowner Guide · Gutters in South Florida

South Florida is hard on gutters. Here's how to keep up.

Around 60 inches of rain a year, palm fronds in every month, and storms that can drop inches in an hour — your gutters here do a job they simply don't do up north. This guide covers what the weather actually does to them, what to check from the ground, and how to tell when cleaning stops being enough.

The climate problem

What the rainy season does to a gutter system

Start with the math of your roof. A 2,000-square-foot roof in a one-inch rain sheds more than 1,200 gallons of water — and South Florida's summer storms regularly deliver that inch in under an hour, several days a week, from June through October. Your gutters are the only thing standing between that water and your fascia, your soffit, your walls, and your foundation.

When gutters are clean and pitched right, the system handles it. When they're partly blocked — and here, "partly blocked" can happen six weeks after a cleaning — the water has to go somewhere:

  • Over the front edge, in a sheet that soaks the ground below, washes out mulch and soil, and stains the walk or pool deck.
  • Over the back edge, against the fascia board and into the soffit — the quiet overflow that does the expensive damage.
  • Nowhere. Water standing in a clogged gutter is heavy. It pulls spikes and hangers loose, the run sags, the sag holds more water, and the cycle feeds itself. Standing water is also where mosquitoes breed — in a clogged gutter, they're breeding on your roofline.

None of this needs a hurricane. Ordinary rainy-season Tuesdays do it, one overflow at a time.

Know your debris

What's actually filling your gutters

Your cleaning schedule is set by your trees, not by a calendar. The mix around homes in places like Coral Springs, Parkland, and Weston — heavy canopy, big lots — is different from a beachside roofline, and it fills gutters at a different rate.

  • Palm fronds. Long, stiff, and dropped year-round. A frond doesn't flow to the downspout — it bridges across the gutter and dams everything behind it.
  • Oak leaves and spring catkins. Live oaks drop leaves and then, in spring, release catkins that mat down into a dense paste when wet. That paste seals gutter bottoms and guard openings alike.
  • Seed pods and fruit. Mahogany pods, palm seeds, and similar drop is heavy for its size and collects at corners and outlets.
  • Roof grit. Tile and shingle roofs shed sediment constantly. Grit settles in the bottom of the gutter, adds real weight, and holds moisture against the metal.

If your roofline sits under two or more of these, assume your gutters fill faster than the "twice a year" rule of thumb you may have heard from someone in a drier state.

The damage chain

From clog to rot: how the damage spreads

The reason gutter people talk about fascia so much is that the chain is predictable. Overflow soaks the fascia board behind the gutter. Paint fails, wood stays wet, and rot starts — usually hidden behind the gutter itself, so the first visible sign is often paint bubbling or the gutter pulling away because its screws no longer have solid wood to hold.

Water that reaches the soffit does the same work overhead: panels stain, sag, and open at the seams. Open soffit is an invitation — wasps build in it, and in South Florida it's a known entry route for rodents and raccoons into the attic. Meanwhile the overflow that misses the house entirely lands at the foundation line, washing out soil, undermining pavers, and feeding the damp that stucco and paint then have to fight.

The pattern to remember: by the time gutter damage is obvious from inside the house, it's been getting wet for months. The checks below exist to catch it while it's still cheap.

The ground-level check

Five things to check — no ladder required

You can learn most of what matters about your gutters from the yard, especially during and right after rain.

  1. 1

    Watch it rain

    During a real downpour, walk the house. Overflow points tell you exactly where the clogs are — water spilling over the front edge mid-run means a blockage between there and the downspout.

  2. 2

    Read the streaks

    Dark tide lines on the fascia or streaks down the siding below a gutter run are a written record of past overflows, even on a dry day.

  3. 3

    Check the downspout ends

    In rain, a healthy downspout discharges hard. A trickle — or water backing up at the seams above it — means the blockage is up top.

  4. 4

    Look for sags and sprouts

    A run that dips between hangers is holding water. Anything green growing out of a gutter is a clog with roots. Spikes or screws visibly backing out of the fascia mean the wood behind them may already be soft.

  5. 5

    Walk the drip line after storms

    Fresh washouts in the mulch, new puddles against the foundation, or a downspout section hanging at a new angle after a storm — photograph them. Photos make any later conversation with a contractor shorter and more honest.

After a storm

The post-storm checklist

After a named storm or any night of serious wind, add three checks to the list above. First, debris dams at corners and valleys — wind moves fronds and branches to exactly the spots where water turns. Second, branches resting on the roof edge: they hold debris and moisture against the gutter line even if the gutters themselves are clean. Third, new overflow marks — a streak that wasn't there last month means something shifted, clogged, or let go.

One safety note that matters more than any of this: don't climb onto a wet roof or a tall ladder on wet ground to investigate. Everything on this page can be assessed from the yard, and anything that can't is a job for someone with the right equipment. That's true whether you end up calling me or anyone else.

The honest math

When cleaning stops being enough

Cleaning works. It just works on a subscription you never stop paying — in money if you hire it out, in ladder time if you don't — and it only takes one skipped season under a heavy canopy for the damage chain above to get started. For a one-story home with a mostly clear roofline, regular cleaning can be a perfectly sensible plan.

Guards start paying off when some combination of these is true: you're cleaning two or more times a year; the home is two stories, where cleaning is riskier and pricier; the roofline sits under palms or oaks; or you're simply done climbing and want the problem closed. The other half of the decision is choosing the guard itself — the types differ a lot, and some fail here in specific, predictable ways. Questions

South Florida gutters, answered

How often should gutters be cleaned here?

It depends on your tree canopy. A home with a clear roofline might need cleaning once a year; a home under palms or oaks can need it three or four times a year. If water is overflowing during storms, the schedule has already fallen behind — overflow, not the calendar, is the real signal.

Can clogged gutters really damage the house?

Yes — the damage just starts out of sight. Overflow runs down the fascia and soaks the soffit, which rots the wood and opens gaps that pests use. Water that misses the downspout pools at the foundation and washes out soil and landscaping. Most of it is preventable, and most of it is cheaper to prevent than to repair.

Do guards mean I never touch my gutters again?

A quality guard system ends the routine of scooping out clogged gutters, and that is the honest headline. It does not make the roofline immortal: a ground-level look once a year — checking for sagging runs, loose downspouts, and storm damage — is still smart. Anyone who tells you any system needs zero attention forever is selling, not informing.
